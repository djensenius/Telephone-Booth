## Research

### Research approach and source note

This document distinguishes between two kinds of evidence:

- **Project-supplied oral history:** information provided by a reliable source
  closely connected to the garden's founding.
- **Published and archival sources:** the garden's website, municipal and GRCA
  material, reporting, archived websites, funding records, and archival
  catalogues.

The 2000 parking-lot proposal is treated as established oral history. It may
have been an informal or preliminary proposal, because no separately titled
parking report has been found in the searchable municipal records.

Some public sources disagree about dates, counts, and terminology. Those
differences are preserved below rather than silently resolved.

### Site overview

The [Cambridge Sculpture Garden][our-story] is a narrow public garden beside
the Grand River in historic Galt. It occupies part of the Living Levee and
Trans Canada Trail system and sits opposite the former Goldie & McCulloch
industrial complex, later Southworks and now part of the Gaslight District.

The garden officially launched in September 2000 through cooperation among
community volunteers, the City of Cambridge, and the Grand River Conservation
Authority. It is free and open at all times.

The Cambridge Sculpture Garden is a registered charity governed by
volunteers. Seasonal or part-time professional gardeners perform some
maintenance, so "volunteer-managed" describes its governance rather than
every hour of labour.

### Indigenous histories and continuing communities

There was not one single "Indigenous community" that can be assigned to the
garden parcel across all periods. Different peoples lived in, travelled
through, harvested from, governed, and maintained relationships with the
Grand River valley over thousands of years.

The garden's [land acknowledgement][garden-land-ack], written by Rick Monture,
states that Indigenous peoples have inhabited the land for at least 10,000
years. The Grand River's Canadian Heritage River nomination identified 820
confirmed Indigenous archaeological sites across the watershed, representing
occupation from approximately 9000 BCE to 1650 CE. These included 66 villages
and 169 camps.

Those watershed-wide findings demonstrate sustained Indigenous presence along
the river, but they do not establish that a village or camp stood on the exact
53–67 Grand Avenue parcel. No parcel-specific archaeological assessment has
been located during this research.

The garden acknowledgement identifies several cultural and national
relationships in the Cambridge area:

- The Iroquoian-speaking Attawandaron, also called the Neutral Nation.
- The Huron-Wendat.
- The Haudenosaunee.
- The Algonquian-speaking Anishinaabe peoples, including the Mississaugas.

A [joint historical overview][six-nations-mcfn-overview] produced by Six
Nations of the Grand River and the Mississaugas of the Credit explains that
before the eighteenth century the area was the traditional territory of the
Huron-Wendat and Neutral Nations and was also known to and used by the
Haudenosaunee. Following seventeenth-century conflicts, many Neutral people
were incorporated into the Seneca Nation rather than simply disappearing
from history.

The same overview describes a shared-use agreement between the Haudenosaunee
and Anishinabek around 1701, remembered as the Dish With One Spoon. The
agreement concerned peaceful sharing of land and resources without treating
them as an unlimited possession.

The river should therefore be understood as a living cultural landscape and
not merely a scenic boundary. It supported movement, fishing, hunting,
harvesting, trade, gathering, and settlement long before it became an
industrial water source, flood-control channel, trail, or sculpture-garden
setting.

#### Treaty and land-rights context

The treaty history is overlapping and remains contested; it should not be
presented as a simple transfer from one people to another.

The [Mississaugas of the Credit First Nation][mcfn-between-lakes] describes
Cambridge as lying within the Between the Lakes Purchase. In 1784 the British
Crown negotiated with the Mississaugas for lands between Lakes Huron,
Ontario, and Erie. Boundary errors led to a confirming agreement in 1792.

Later in 1784, the Haldimand Proclamation authorized Mohawk people and other
members of the Six Nations who had lost homes after the American Revolution
to settle along the Grand River. The described tract extended six miles on
each side of the river from its mouth to its source.

[Six Nations Lands and Resources][six-nations-haldimand] describes this as a
tract of approximately 950,000 acres that Six Nations and their descendants
were to enjoy forever. It reports that less than five per cent remains as the
present Six Nations reserve and continues to research alleged unlawful
alienation and mismanagement of Haldimand Tract lands.

The City of Cambridge's settler history begins the town narrative with William
Dickson coming into possession of approximately 90,000 acres in 1816 and
developing Shade's Mills, later Galt. That narrative should be read alongside,
not instead of, the continuing Six Nations land-rights history and the
Mississaugas' treaty history.

Six Nations of the Grand River and the Mississaugas of the Credit First
Nation remain living communities with active cultural, political, legal, and
stewardship relationships to the watershed. Indigenous history at the garden
is not only ancient or commemorative.

#### Implications for interpretation

The site's public story should not begin with the workers' cottages, the 1974
flood, or the 2000 garden campaign. Those are later layers within a much
longer river history.

The existing acknowledgement provides a foundation, but interpretation could
go further by:

- Including Indigenous voices in future signs, audio, questions, and
  commissions.
- Explaining why the Grand River itself is culturally significant.
- Distinguishing the Attawandaron, Huron-Wendat, Haudenosaunee, and
  Anishinaabe histories rather than collapsing them into a generic category.
- Describing the Between the Lakes and Haldimand histories together.
- Acknowledging that Haldimand Tract land rights remain active and contested.
- Presenting Six Nations and Mississaugas of the Credit as present-day
  communities rather than only as people from the past.

### Before the sculpture garden

#### Workers' cottages and industry

The riverfront was once a working residential landscape rather than vacant
open space. Approximately a dozen workers' cottages occupied the wider
stretch of Grand Avenue opposite the Goldie & McCulloch foundry. The garden's
history identifies eight cottages as being removed from the area affected by
the raised flood-control grade.

Railway cars were shunted near the cottages on their way to the industrial
complex. The Grand Oak stood in the backyard of one of the houses, probably
63 Grand Avenue South, although the exact address still requires
confirmation.

The different cottage counts can coexist: approximately a dozen may have
formed the larger neighbourhood, while eight were directly associated with
the present garden or its section of the levee.

The "Tigger House cottages" mentioned in a separate 1997 heritage inventory
are almost certainly unrelated. Tigger House was an employer-supported
daycare at 27 Grand Avenue South, north of the garden's original 53–67 Grand
Avenue lease area.

#### Floods and riverbank reconstruction

Photographs show the site and oak surrounded by floodwater in 1947. The
catastrophic Grand River flood of May 17, 1974 triggered the modern
flood-protection project.

The work occurred in phases rather than as a single construction event:

- Flood-control work began by 1975–76.
- The intervention that preserved the Grand Oak dates to approximately 1976.
- Regional heritage material dates a major Living Levee segment to 1978.
- GRCA building inventories from 1979, 1983–84, and 1986 document properties,
  photographs, maps, demolition dates, legal arrangements, and correspondence
  with owners.
- Technical and governmental flood-control records continued through at
  least the mid-1980s.
- The 2007 Living Levee Plan later reframed the engineered riverbank as a
  linked system of open space, recreation, and heritage interpretation.

The garden therefore rests on the result of more than a decade of demolition,
engineering, property negotiation, and landscape reconstruction.

As of October 2, 2026, the City reports that a section of the Living Levee
Trail between Concession Street Bridge and Barradell's Loft is permanently
closed because of flood damage. The flood-protection landscape remains
vulnerable to the river it was designed to manage.

### The Grand Oak

The [Grand Oak][grand-oak] is a white oak dating to approximately 1880,
making it about 145–150 years old in 2026. It was one of only two mature trees
on the otherwise flat parcel when the garden was proposed.

During levee construction, City forester John Kingswood advocated for saving
the tree. Workers installed perforated pipes over its original root zone and
built a well around the trunk before adding roughly five feet of fill. More
than a metre of the trunk now sits below the visible garden grade.

A removable platform allows inspection, watering, and fertilizing around the
buried base. Its opening has periodically been enlarged as the trunk grows.
The tree's survival is therefore both biological and engineered.

There are two heritage milestones:

- In 2008, Judy Welsh initiated a request for heritage recognition, and the
  tree received recognition through an Ontario urban-forestry or heritage-tree
  program.
- In 2010, City of Cambridge Bylaw 142-10 legally designated the Grand Oak as
  a property of cultural heritage significance under the Ontario Heritage
  Act.

The oak is the only individually designated heritage tree in Cambridge. In
2023 it was a finalist in Tree Trust's Ontario Tree of the Year program.
Some garden material describes it as having received the award, while
contemporary reporting describes it as a finalist.

### Parking-lot proposal and grassroots campaign

According to the project-supplied oral history, the riverside parcel was flat
land being proposed for a parking lot in 2000.

Judy Welsh and Heather Franklin led a grassroots effort that persuaded the
City of Cambridge and GRCA to permit a sculpture garden instead. Welsh was a
landscape designer whose professional contacts helped obtain soil, trees,
shrubs, grass, mulch, plants, equipment, and labour. Franklin brought
experience in arts administration, public committees, and community
organizations.

The speed of the physical transformation may have had political importance
as well as aesthetic value. Turning the parcel into a visibly used public
garden within weeks made the alternative to parking tangible and potentially
harder to reverse. This is an interpretation worth testing with the founders
and early volunteers.

### The Galt Horticultural Society connection

The sculpture garden was initially established through the Galt Horticultural
Society rather than through an independent Cambridge Sculpture Garden legal
entity.

The relationship was both institutional and spatial:

- Ferguson Cottage, the Horticultural Society's base, is at 37 Grand Avenue
  South.
- City-owned land used as the Horticultural Society garden is associated with
  45 Grand Avenue South.
- The original sculpture-garden lease covered Lots 18–22 and part of Lot 17,
  described as 53–67 Grand Avenue South.

A 2004 Canadian Heritage Rivers report lists the organization as
"Cambridge Sculpture Garden – Galt Horticultural Society." Heather Franklin
was involved with both organizations, strengthening the connection between
horticulture, contemporary art, and municipal processes.

The public legal trail is:

| Date | Record | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| January 10, 2000 | Planning and Development Committee report CL2-00 | Proposed a lease between the City and Galt Horticultural Society for sculpture gardens on Grand Avenue South |
| January 20, 2000 | City lease catalogue record | Describes a 24-sheet lease covering 53–67 Grand Avenue South |
| January 31, 2000 | Council Meeting 3-00 | Included the sculpture-garden lease agreement in its agenda and reports |
| March 20, 2000 | Bylaw 52/00 | Authorized execution of the lease with the Galt Horticultural Society |
| 2006 | Organizational history | Cambridge Sculpture Garden became an independent registered charity |

The current land tenure and any later replacement for the original lease
remain unclear.

### Rapid landscape transformation

The [official history][our-story] records a large amount of donated material
and volunteer labour:

- Twenty-two truckloads of soil created the berms and contours.
- Connon Nurseries supplied shrubs.
- Compact Sod donated sod and installation labour.
- A TD Friends of the Environment grant supported native planting.
- Members of 296 Royal Canadian Air Cadets planted more than 150 native
  shrubs and grasses.

The Air Cadets and the Galt Horticultural Society's Cambridge Sculpture
Garden were separately listed among Cambridge Millennium Project grant
applicants. Their work at the garden may have been coordinated as part of a
broader Millennium initiative.

The garden launched in September 2000, while a site photograph dates
recontouring work to 2001. The landscape therefore developed rapidly but not
in a single day.

### Founders and early volunteers

Judy Welsh combined organizing with landscape design and remains publicly
identified as a founder. She continued to lead garden-design tours and public
programming in 2026.

[Heather Franklin][heather-franklin] brought experience from the Cambridge
Arts Centre, Cambridge Galleries, arts committees, the Galt Horticultural
Society, and other cultural organizations. Her later biography explicitly
identifies her as a Cambridge Sculpture Garden founder.

An archived 2007 contact page identifies:

- Judy Welsh for general inquiries.
- Judy Major-Girardin for sculpture exhibitions.
- Chris Barrows for donations.
- Gerten Basom and Tao Basom in organizational and web roles.

Judy Major-Girardin has remained involved for more than two decades, providing
significant continuity in exhibitions and public programming.

### Landscape gallery and ecology

The organization describes the site as a [landscape gallery][landscape-gallery]
rather than a park containing unrelated objects. Berms, paths, planting beds,
river views, and sight lines shape how each sculpture is encountered.

The current website says approximately half the garden is planted with native
plants and grasses. A 2009 brochure said approximately one-third. The
difference may reflect later expansion or a changed method of calculation.

The garden reports that it uses no pesticides or herbicides. Its
[planting inventory][blooming-garden] includes white and English oaks, tulip
tree, locust, ginkgo, spruce, sumacs, dogwoods, chokeberry, ninebark, roses,
salvias, coneflowers, sedums, woodland plants, and ornamental grasses.

This produces an unresolved but productive tension between ecological
restoration, ornamental horticulture, flood infrastructure, and the visual
requirements of an outdoor gallery.

### Permanent art collection

The current [permanent-art page][permanent-art] describes seven permanent
sculptures and one mural.

| Work | Artist | History and relationship to the site |
|---|---|---|
| *Twilight* | Andreas Gehr | Commissioned by Toronto Harbourfront in 1984, shown in Cambridge in 1985, displayed at GRCA headquarters, and installed as the garden's first permanent sculpture in 2000 |
| *Ten Untitled Sculptures* | Peter Bowyer | Ten-part constructivist group made in 1981 and installed in 2004 |
| *Easy to Assemble* | John Marriott | A destabilized garden shed that plays with domestic, suburban, and prefabricated forms; sources disagree whether it dates to 2005–06 or 2007–08 |
| *Silver Key* | Dave Hind | Reclaimed-steel work installed in 2010 |
| *Gabion Tower* | Gareth Lichty | Installed in 2012 using erosion-control gabion forms to depict a southern Ontario water tower; originally planned as a temporary two-year work |
| *Roses* | Andrea Manica | Mural depicting people smelling flowers and emphasizing attention, transience, and ordinary encounters |
| *Green Pentagonal* | Laura Marotta with Brian Redmond | Five-foot sphere of 60 stainless-steel pentagonal faces arranged in 12 florets; installed in June 2025 and identified as the first permanent sculpture by a woman |
| *Toad Touch* | Annie Dunning | Touchable bronze installed in August 2026; repeated contact is intended to alter its patina |

The collection count is inconsistent. The website says seven permanent
sculptures plus one mural, while August 2026 reporting described *Toad Touch*
as the eighth permanent sculpture. The difference may come from counting the
mural or the ten Bowyer elements differently, or from an outdated page.

The garden's first permanent sculpture was made 16 years before the garden
opened. *Twilight* connects the site to Cambridge's earlier experiments with
temporary public sculpture and to GRCA's cultural stewardship.

The collection also reveals a representation gap. The first permanent work
by a woman was not installed until 2025; the second followed in 2026.

### Temporary art and programming

The garden says it has presented more than 20 temporary sculptures, although
its online archive is incomplete and several entries lack dates, titles, or
interpretive text.

Documented examples include:

- Scott McNicol's *A Question of Who's in Charge* in 2000.
- Ryszard Litwiniuk's *11 from Two* in 2000.
- Max Streicher's inflatable giant babies.
- Alan Flint's *Use*.
- Gilgore Stefanov's *Nest*.
- Simon Frank's *Bur*.
- Mary Catherine Newcomb's *Souvenir*.
- Maria Simmons, Dean Hossack, and Amanda Drinkwater's bee hotels.
- Sarah Peebles's *Sonic Solitaries* or Audio Bee Cabinet.
- Ted Fullerton's *Axis Mundi*.
- Susan Detwiler's *Seed Ball*.
- Rebecca Diedrich's *Blue Bias*.
- Niall Donaghy's *Monopoly*.
- Gil McElroy's *Memory Tables*.
- A community *Wrapped Cottage* quilt installation.
- Laura Marotta's *Garden Shelter*.
- Ernest Daetwyler's *Boat Project/everythingwillbefine* in 2021–22.

The garden has partnered with CAFKA and has hosted children's art activities,
music, dance, tours, artist talks, and community events. Its archived 2007
history describes children's programming throughout the year and outdoor
performance by guest artists.

Permanent works are assessed for durability, public safety, compatibility
with an always-open site, and their ability to complement the landscape.
Temporary work allows more experimental material and form.

### Governance, labour, and funding

The garden is a charitable organization governed by volunteers on publicly
controlled land. Visitors can easily experience it as an ordinary municipal
park even though much of its programming, fundraising, gardening, insurance,
and art stewardship is carried by a small volunteer organization.

Typical expenses include:

- Liability insurance.
- Seasonal gardening labour.
- Plants, mulch, and soil.
- Irrigation operation and repair.
- Lighting and signage.
- Sculpture installation and maintenance.
- Stormwater and other property-related charges.

A 2022 federal tourism grant of $30,000 supported interpretive and visitor
improvements including sculpture labels, QR-linked audio and video, lighting,
entrance signage, an Indigenous land acknowledgement, Riverwalk banners, a
mural, a bench, and renewed garden beds.

The City awarded $16,000 in 2025. A 2026 application requested $23,000; City
staff recommended $10,000. These records illustrate the mismatch between the
garden's public role and its limited operating base.

The phrase "managed entirely by volunteers" should therefore be understood as
volunteer governance supported by some paid seasonal or specialist work.

### Surrounding urban change

The garden's context has changed substantially since 2000:

- Southworks ceased operating as an outlet mall, and the former industrial
  complex became part of the Gaslight District redevelopment.
- The pedestrian bridge opened in 2018, linking the garden and west riverbank
  to Founder's Point and the east side of the Grand.
- The Living Levee has become part of a broader walking and cycling network.
- New housing and visitor traffic have increased the garden's visibility.

The garden now acts as a connector among downtown Galt, the river, industrial
heritage, trails, housing, public art, and cultural destinations rather than
as an isolated pocket park.

### Documentation differences and unanswered questions

Known differences among public sources include:

- **Parking proposal:** accepted as reliable oral history but not found as a
  separately titled council report.
- **Cottage count:** eight on the garden site versus approximately a dozen in
  the wider immediate neighbourhood.
- **Grand Oak recognition:** a 2008 request or external recognition versus
  legal municipal designation in 2010.
- **Tree award:** some garden material says Tree of the Year; contemporary
  reporting says finalist.
- **Native planting:** one-third in 2009 versus one-half today.
- ***Easy to Assemble* dates:** 2005–06 in an older brochure versus 2007–08
  on the current site.
- **Permanent collection count:** seven sculptures plus one mural versus
  reporting that calls *Toad Touch* the eighth permanent sculpture.
- **GRCA environmental award:** GRCA records point to 2004, while an older
  garden brochure refers to 2005.

Important unanswered questions include:

- What exact parking plan was proposed, and who proposed it?
- Which cottage addresses occupied 53–67 Grand Avenue South?
- Who lived in the cottages immediately before demolition?
- Which cottage had the Grand Oak in its backyard?
- Did any cottages remain after the first levee phase?
- Where are Judy Welsh's original landscape drawings?
- Who supplied the 22 truckloads of soil and heavy equipment?
- What were the original lease term, rent, maintenance obligations, and GRCA
  permissions?
- When and how did the independent charity replace the Galt Horticultural
  Society as the operating body?
- What is the complete chronology of temporary exhibitions?
- How should workers' housing, Indigenous history, and flood-control
  engineering be interpreted on site?
